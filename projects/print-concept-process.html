From plant study to final jaal

How a single climbing plant became a bel, a buta and a jaal, and how that jaal was reworked for a painting and for a brand.

Plant Study

The project began with Poranopsis paniculata, known as bridal bouquet or snow creeper, a woody climber from the morning glory family. Its tendrils, small white flowers and heart shaped leaves became the botanical reference for everything that follows.

Plant
Poranopsis paniculata, family Convolvulaceae
Habit
Woody climber or liana
Leaves
Ovate, heart shaped, 4 to 9 cm long, velvety
Flowers
Small, white, funnel shaped, in large panicles
Native range
The Himalayas, in dense mixed forests and open scrub
Flowering
Autumn to winter, October to January

Bel, Buta and Jaal Iterations

The plant was drawn out as a bel, a buta and a jaal. The first sketches tested how the vine could carry rhythm across a surface, how a single sprig could stand alone as a buta, and how the same stem could run as a border.

New Bel, Buta and Jaal

After the necessary edits the three elements were redrawn to size and placed together in a layout, to see how density, border and buta sit beside each other on cloth.

Rendering

Two rendering styles were tried on the same drawing before any colour came in: a dashed, stitched line that keeps the surface light, and a stippled ground that pushes the motif forward in white.

Art Inspiration

For the Print Concept course the jaal was developed again, this time in answer to contemporary Indian art: the painting Weeping Grace by Jayasri Burman. Her work has a dreamlike, lyrical quality and draws on Indian mythology, nature and the celebration of feminine strength.


The painting was chosen for its immediate visual intrigue and its complexity. The dense arrangement of motifs is a rich source for a complex jaal.

Aesthetics
Lyrical, dreamlike, intricate, layered, mythical
Colour and tone
Vibrant, jewel toned, rich, textured
Theme
Feminine strength, nature rooted, emotional depth, narrative

Trials and Errors

The jaal went through five colour trials. Each one fixed something the last one got wrong.

Brand Study: The Shop

The Shop is a brand known for translating traditional textile aesthetics into contemporary, refined design. Its classic collection uses very traditional jaal patterns with modern elements worked in.


The motifs are intricate and detailed, with a thin outline and layers that add depth without weight. The palette prefers pastel shades set against bright and earthy tones. The overall feel is delicate and minimal, with an understated luxury.
